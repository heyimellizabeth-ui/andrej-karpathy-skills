# Digital Product Validator

A working method for judging and shaping a digital product idea before anyone builds it. Distilled from the tactical parts of a multi-day "AI income" training; the motivational and sales material was discarded.

**Caveat on the source:** it was a sales event. Its frameworks are sound marketing practice, but its earnings figures are unverified marketing claims. Never repeat them as evidence, and never promise the user income.

## 1. Check it is a digital product

It qualifies if it is **created digitally and delivered digitally**: no stock, no shipping, no per-unit work. Sell the recipe, not the cake.

Formats, simplest first:

1. **Ebook / guide / PDF** (can be 5 pages)
2. **Digital asset**: template, spreadsheet, planner, dashboard, printable
3. **Pre-recorded program**: video or audio course
4. **Interactive assistant**: a chatbot trained on a body of material

Start at 1 or 2. Only move up a level once the simpler version is selling; the same content can be upgraded later.

## 2. Score the idea on five criteria

Score each 1-10 and say why. A weak score on Pain cannot be rescued by the others.

| Criterion | Question | Signals |
|---|---|---|
| **Pain** | Does it remove a real problem, not add a nice-to-have? | People move away from pain far more readily than toward pleasure. Test: how often is the person *reminded* of the problem? A toothache (every meal, every night) beats whiter teeth (occasionally). Strongest areas: money, health, relationships. Hobbies are usually pleasure-driven and harder. |
| **Worsening** | Does the problem get worse if ignored? | Escalating problems create urgency (failing car battery); static ones don't (windscreen chip). Mostly decided by the pain you picked. |
| **Transformation** | Is there a clear point A to point B? | Buyers pay for the change, not the page count. |
| **Specificity** | Would the right person think "this is about me"? | See below. |
| **Speed** | Is it the shortest credible path to the result? | 20 pages that get there beat 200 that also get there. The time claim must be believable. |

### Mapping the transformation

Write point A before point B. Point A is not just the problem; it covers:

- how the person **feels**
- what they **fear**
- what they **avoid**
- how they **see themselves**

Point B then follows almost automatically. Goal: describe their problem better than they could themselves. One topic contains several distinct transformations ("play football without knee pain" and "shoot with more power" are different products for different people).

### Testing specificity

Narrow in steps: *parents* → *dads* → *divorced dads with teenage sons*. Each step shrinks the audience and raises relevance.

- A qualifier only counts if it excludes people. "Busy moms" narrows nothing, since all moms are busy. Name the distinguishing problem instead.
- Stop narrowing while plenty of people still have the problem. Specific enough to feel personal, common enough to exist in numbers.
- Small audiences are fine: a few dozen buyers of a recurring product can be meaningful income.

## 3. Write the product promise

One sentence, four parts:

> I help **[specific person]** with **[painful, worsening problem]** get **[result]** in **[realistic time]**.

Example: *I help first-time parents of 4-6 month olds whose nights get harder every week get their baby sleeping through the night in seven nights.*

If the sentence can't be written cleanly, the idea isn't ready. Go back to step 2.

## 4. Build for the result, not for volume

- Deliver an **action plan**, not a survey of the topic. The value is in sparing the buyer the research and the contradictory advice.
- Resist padding. Adding more usually lowers value by slowing the path to the result.
- Prefer a **proven, simple** problem over a novel idea. Originality is not the goal; a solved problem is.
- Look for ideas in problems the people around you already have, and in questions an existing audience keeps asking.

## 5. Sanity-check the opportunity

Three questions before investing time:

1. **Is there already demand**, or would people have to be convinced they need it?
2. **What does it cost if it fails?** As a number, not a feeling.
3. **Who is already doing it**, and are they people like the user, or people with advantages the user lacks? Compare against ordinary first-timers, not the headline success stories.

Operating rules the source was consistent on:

- Keep the cost per attempt near zero so the user can keep trying. Do not spend on ads or borrow money to start.
- Build it alongside existing work or study. Do not quit income to do this.
- Stick with one business model. Expect several months of little or nothing.

## 6. Plan distribution and delivery

**Distribute:** people rarely go looking for a digital product; it gets placed in front of them where they already gather. Identify the existing communities or audiences where the specific person from step 3 already spends time, rather than planning to "sell" cold.

**Deliver:** fulfilment must be automatic so 10 sales and 10,000 sales cost the same effort. A hosting/checkout platform should offer:

- payments that work in the user's country, with payouts that won't be frozen
- one-time, subscription, and instalment pricing
- automatic access on purchase (no emailing files by hand)
- a sales dashboard
- no per-product listing fee

**Presentation:** purchases rest on trust, and design is part of trust. Let AI produce most of the design; a human reviews and corrects.

**Pricing observations** (anecdotal, not rules): simple guides sat around $15-$70; recurring memberships $25-$250/month; bundling direct access to the creator moved one product from ~$200 to ~$1,500 and reportedly drew fewer, more committed buyers. Raising value can work better than cutting price.

## Guardrails

- **No income promises.** Report the scores and the reasoning, not expected earnings.
- **Credible claims only.** Flag any result or timeframe in a product promise that a buyer couldn't realistically achieve.
- **High-stakes topics need real expertise.** For health, medical, legal, or financial products, AI-generated content must be reviewed by someone qualified before sale. Say so plainly; "you need no knowledge of the topic" is not acceptable there.
- **Don't fill gaps with invention.** The source did not include its method for researching profitable topics or its specific distribution channels. If asked for those, reason from first principles and say that is what you are doing.
